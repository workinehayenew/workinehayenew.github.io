### Publications

**[Armed conflict exposure and violent discipline of children at home: Panel evidence from Ethiopia](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.socscimed.2026.119976)**<br>
with Andreas Kotsadam, Charlotte Ringdal and Espen Villanger<br>
*Social Science & Medicine*, 411 (2026), 119976<br>
Working paper version: [HiCN Working Paper 451](https://hicn.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/HiCN-WP-451.pdf)

<details>
<summary><strong>Abstract</strong></summary>
<p>Armed conflict has far-reaching effects on household well-being, including increased risk of violence. We examine whether changes in local armed conflict exposure are associated with changes in caregiver-perpetrated physical punishment of children, as reported by mothers. We link six waves (2016–2021) of panel survey data from employed women in Ethiopia (1065 respondents; just over 5000 respondent-wave observations) to georeferenced conflict events from ACLED. Using respondent and wave fixed-effects linear probability models, we find that a one-standard-deviation increase in conflict exposure (19.4 events) increases the probability of physical punishment by any caregiver by 3.4 percentage points (pp) (≈5.2% relative to the mean), father punishment by 2.7 pp (≈6.4%), mother punishment by 3.6 pp (≈5.6%), and punishment by both caregivers by 2.9 pp (≈7.3%). A decomposition into presence versus intensity indicates a sizeable increase at conflict onset, with smaller incremental increases as events accumulate. Conflict exposure also coincides with higher caregiver distress and indicators of economic strain, including reduced labor supply and lower reported income among fathers. These findings suggest that conflict can spill over into harsher parenting, highlighting the importance of integrating child protection, caregiver mental health support, and livelihood assistance into conflict-response programming.</p>
</details>

**[War comes home: Conflict exposure and intimate partner violence in Ethiopia](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.socscimed.2026.119866)**<br>
with Andreas Kotsadam, Charlotte Ringdal and Espen Villanger<br>
*Social Science & Medicine*, 409 (2026), 119866<br>
Working paper version: [CMI Working Paper WP 2026:3](https://www.cmi.no/publications/9994-war-comes-home-conflict-exposure-and-intimate-partner-violence-in-ethiopia)

<details>
<summary><strong>Abstract</strong></summary>
<p>Armed conflict can increase risks of violence within households, including intimate partner violence (IPV), that is, physical, sexual, or psychological harm inflicted by a current or former partner. Existing evidence links conflict exposure to IPV, but little is known about how IPV risk scales with the intensity of nearby violence, whether conflict exacerbates pre-existing IPV, contributes to new IPV, or both, and the household-level mechanisms that may underlie these associations. We examine these questions in Ethiopia, a country affected by recent large-scale conflict. We link geolocated conflict events from the Armed Conflict Location &amp; Event Data (ACLED) to six waves of household panel data collected across five regions between 2016 and 2021, and estimate individual fixed-effects models. A one standard deviation increase in local conflict events is associated with a 1.6 percentage point (pp) increase in the probability of reporting any IPV, a 1.1 pp increase in the probability of physical IPV, and a 1.1 pp increase in the probability of sexual IPV, corresponding to 7%, 11%, and 33% of the respective outcome means. The association rises with conflict intensity: each additional conflict event predicts higher IPV, whereas the presence of an isolated event does not. Associations are concentrated among women reporting no lifetime IPV at baseline. Conflict exposure predicts higher psychological distress in both partners, reduced working hours, lower husband income, and greater acceptance of wife-beating. The findings suggest that IPV prevention, integrated with mental health and livelihood support, belongs in the core of responses to escalating conflict.</p>
</details>
